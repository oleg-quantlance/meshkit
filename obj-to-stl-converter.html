What the Online OBJ to STL Converter Does

Wavefront OBJ is the lingua franca of 3D modelling, scanning and game art: Blender, Maya, ZBrush, Meshroom, Polycam and most photogrammetry apps export it. It stores vertices (v), faces (f) that can be triangles, quads or arbitrary polygons, plus normals, texture coordinates and material references that slicers do not use. STL — what every slicer, print service and mesh repair tool expects — stores triangles only. This converter reads the OBJ in your browser, fan-triangulates every polygon, resolves relative (negative) indices, merges all objects and groups into one mesh, applies the unit and up-axis you choose, and writes a binary or ASCII STL with recomputed facet normals.

Because OBJ has no units, the two options that matter most are units and up axis. Blender and most game pipelines write metres with Y up; CAD exporters write millimetres with Z up. Choose correctly here and the part lands in your slicer at the right size, standing on the bed. The mesh is audited for open edges and inverted winding before download, and nothing is uploaded — the file is parsed in a Web Worker on your machine (100 MB limit).

How to Convert OBJ to STL in 3 Steps

  1. Drop the .obj: Only the .obj file is needed; the companion .mtl and textures are ignored because STL cannot carry colour.
  2. Set units and up axis: If the bounding box in the log reads 0.05 × 0.03 × 0.02 mm, your file is in metres — switch to Meters. If the model is lying on its side in the viewer or slicer, choose Y up → Z up.
  3. Export .stl: Binary is the default. The log lists how many faces were polygons, how many triangles came out, and whether the mesh is watertight. Open it in the viewer to confirm orientation.

Worked Example: Fan Triangulation and Index Resolution

f 1 2 3 4  →  triangles (1,2,3) and (1,3,4)  ·  an n-gon with k corners becomes k − 2 triangles
f -3/1/1 -2/2/1 -1/3/1  →  negative indices count back from the last vertex written so far
Sample vase: 193 vertices, 192 faces (168 quads + 24 cap triangles) → 360 STL facets, open top = 24 boundary edges
Blender default export: metres, Y up  →  choose Meters + Y up to get a 64 mm vase standing upright

Fan triangulation is exact for convex, planar polygons — which is what nearly every exporter writes. A non-planar or concave n-gon can produce a self-intersecting fan; if the log reports many open edges on a model that looked closed, re-export with "Triangulate faces" enabled in the source application.

Why Convert OBJ to STL?

  • 3D printing: slicers import OBJ unevenly (some ignore groups, some choke on UVs); STL is the safe path.
  • Scans and sculpts: photogrammetry and ZBrush exports are OBJ by default; STL is what print services quote on.
  • Mesh repair and measurement: MeshKit's repair and volume tools work on STL.
  • Stripping materials: a single geometry file with no dangling .mtl or texture references.

Technical Limitations

Materials, per-vertex colours, texture coordinates and smoothing groups are discarded. Multiple objects become one STL; export each object separately if you need them apart. Free-form geometry (curv, surf, NURBS) and point or line elements are skipped. Faces referencing indices that do not exist are skipped and counted in the log.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my OBJ uploaded anywhere?

No. Parsing, triangulation and STL writing run in a Web Worker in your browser. The only network requests the page makes are for its own assets and the sample file.

My model comes out tiny / huge — what happened?

OBJ has no unit, so the numbers are taken as millimetres by default. A Blender export in metres is 1,000× too small until you pick Meters. Check the bounding box in the log: it should match the real-world size you expect.

The part is lying on its side in my slicer.

The file is Y-up (Blender, Maya, glTF pipelines). Select Y up → Z up; the converter rotates the model so the original Y becomes Z, which is what printers and slicers assume.

Do I need the .mtl file or textures?

No. STL cannot store colour or textures, so the converter ignores mtllib/usemtl and vt records. Only the .obj itself is read.

Why does the log report open edges when the model looked closed?

Either the source genuinely has an opening (the sample vase does — its top is open), or a concave n-gon fan-triangulated badly, or vertices were duplicated along seams by the exporter. Run the STL through STL Repair, which welds coincident vertices and fills small holes.

Reference // Why Units and Up-Axis Matter

OBJ stores bare numbers. Blender exports in metres with Y up by default; CAD tools export millimetres with Z up. A 50 mm part exported from Blender arrives as 0.05 units lying on its side — pick Meters and Y up and it lands upright at 50 mm.
